How the world gets built

Five islands are laid out first, each a cluster of overlapping circles so the coastline comes out lumpy rather than round. Causeways wander between them, so every island can be reached on foot.

Pavements run round the edge of every block, suburb plots included, with gardens behind them. Only the carriageways are paved, so the town sits on the grass rather than on a slab of tarmac. Walk into Doba's doorway to go inside. Press L outdoors to look straight up and stargaze with the naked eye \u2014 fainter than the telescope but no aiming needed, and nothing to earn from it, just the sky. A ladder in the bedroom goes up to the roof, which is the open soil at the top of the pot: three beds where he grows potatoes, carrots and beans, and a water butt. The rim around it is the terracotta of the pot itself, and over the edge is the green of his garden far below. Sow a bed, let it come up, harvest it for food. He carries film for five plates a night, so the shots have to count. Every meal keeps him full for a while, from a third of a day for a boiled egg or buttered toast up to two days for a roast chicken dinner or the veggie stew, and he can be full for two days at most; the stat beside the coins counts the hours down. Raw ingredients will not do. The food shop, which he walks into by pressing E at the door, stocks dry goods, bakery and veg, dairy and a chiller, and he pays as he takes things off the shelves. Fifteen recipes are in the recipe book on the oven: click one to lay its ingredients out from his bag and the fridge, then cook, and eat it at the kitchen table. Once a meal runs out he goes peckish, then hungry, then slows down, and thirty hours past empty he starves. The stat beside the coins says where he stands: peckish, hungry, very hungry, starving, or fed. The desk upstairs has a computer on it, and clicking the desk or the chair sits him down at it: Snaagle for searching the isles, Astropic where he can sell his plates and buy prints of other snappers' (each one says which telescope took it, at what power and under what seeing), photographs taken through the telescope, a Snapmail inbox from neighbours and the council, a sky log telling him whether tonight is worth setting up for, and the news. Downstairs there is a kitchen table, a sofa, a television and the Basic 101 Telescope at the window, a 20mm refractor that reaches about magnitude 7 from his garden, supplied with two eyepieces, 5\u00d7 and 10\u00d7, swapped with E and Q at the eyepiece, with a counter and a fridge round the south-east wall. Open it and you get its fifteen shelves and the five slots in his bag side by side: drag stacks between them, ten to a stack. G shows what he is carrying on its own, wherever he is. Dragging moves a whole stack, right-clicking picks up half of it and shift-clicking picks up a single item, so stacks can be split and merged freely. Switch the television on at the set, then sit on the sofa to watch Snap News; sitting down with it off just sits him down.

a curved flight hugs the west wall up to the bedroom, where the desk with its computer, the chair, the bedside table with the camera and the note on it, and the bed are, with a bedside table beside it, a post-it note stuck to it, and his Snapphone 14 left on the covers — click it, or stand by the bed and press E, and it goes in his pocket. Whatever he is carrying shows above the controls; tap the phone there or press T to look at it, indoors or out. Click the stairs or stand by them and press E. Cloud comes in five levels, from one for a clear sky to five for full overcast, and changes every minute or so. Outdoors you never see it, only its shadow crossing the ground, and Doba darkens as one passes over him; through the eyepiece it passes as grey patches that swallow whatever is behind them, and it greys the window and can shut the telescope down altogether. Snap News reports the real conditions. SnapperTok shows one post at a time: scroll and the next snaps into place, tap the heart to like it and the bubble to read the comments. The television carries Snap News — a fresh headline every few seconds, a ticker along the bottom, and the odd word about a space agency. The whole sky holds a hundred and eighteen stars, always the same ones in the same places, so it can be surveyed patch by patch. Looking through the eyepiece blacks the room out, the way your eyes adapt at the telescope. A camera sits on the desk. Pick it up, look through the telescope after dark and press space to take a photograph; he cannot get into bed still holding it, so click the desk or stand by it and press E to put it back; the roll keeps the last twelve, with the time and the magnification. The sky is a tall rectangle; pan past its edges and there is nothing out there but black. The Basic 101 is aimed by dragging, or with the arrow keys, and is fixed between five and ten times magnification — scroll to zoom, and fainter stars come out as you go in. Doba lives under a Bortle 6.5 sky, so the town's glow washes out most of them and only the brighter ones get through. The sky darkens the further he gets from the towns and villages: press Q at the telescope to pick it up, Y anywhere outdoors to set it down on the grass, E beside it to look and Q again to carry it on. Indoors it only works on its own spot by the window, where E puts it back. Far out on its own country road stands the Snapper Observatory, under a Bortle 2 sky, where snappers gather round their scopes at night and the dome's slit opens after dark; it is marked on the map. Out there the Milk Spill comes out \u2014 the snappers' home galaxy, a pale river of star cloud with a warm core and dark rifts through it. Looking up with L it is a band across the whole sky; through the telescope its glow thins as you zoom in, but the glass breaks it up into faint stars, more of them the bigger the tube and the higher the magnification. From Doba's house the town glow hides it completely. Somewhere off every road there is darker ground still. Nine nebulae are charted, and one galaxy: the Roterda Galaxy, the nearest big galaxy, a fuzzy point to the naked eye. The Pot Nebula, in the middle of The Pot, is the easiest; most of the others are so faint that only the Dooie 100 under a dark sky shows them, as slightly lighter patches. Some are named for their shapes, like the Snapper Nebula and the Ring of Doba, and some only for where they sit. Each eyepiece needs focusing: scroll the mouse wheel at the eyepiece, or pinch and stretch on a phone, until the focus bar turns green. The biggest telescope on Snapperzon is the Dooie 100, a 100mm Newtonian reflector that brings its own red-dot finder and puts four spikes on the brightest stars. Seven planets are charted, each echoing a world of our own: little grey Mernoma, cloudy Venusie, yellow Marzidau with its white polar cap, banded Daubsi, green Saturnapper with its blue rings, Uranoma rolled on its side, and deep blue Neptunie, the faintest, which needs a dark sky or a big tube to find at all. Each has the size and distance of the real planet it echoes, so its size in the eyepiece and how blurred it looks follow from that and from the tube. They behave like real planets: at low power each is only a point, high magnification is needed to see a disc, a bigger tube brings out detail such as Saturnapper's rings and Marzidau's polar cap, and pushing a small tube too far just gives a dim, soft image. Look through it after dark and you get the stars and the moon, turning slowly with the hour, and at night the stars reflect on the water. A green car sits on the driveway: stand by it and press E to get in. E changes up a gear and Q changes down, through reverse, park, and gears one to three — twenty, thirty and forty miles an hour. Leave it in park for three seconds to get out, anywhere he likes; the car stays where he left it. Music is generated as it plays, and changes with where he is: an open pentatonic theme out in the country, a busier one with a walking bass in town, and something slow and warm indoors. After dark it slows and thins out, and driving puts a beat under it. Sound is synthesised in the browser: footsteps that change with the ground, wind that rises with the cloud, birds by day and crickets by night, engines, gear changes, and every tap on the phone. The button in the bar turns it off. Three moons cross the sky: Muon, Vulcan a third its size and molten red, and Frostis a third its size and frozen. Vulcan orbits twenty per cent closer in than Muon and goes round in 182 seconds; Frostis is forty-five per cent further out and takes 445. Further out sits Daubsi \u2014 a gas giant eleven times Doba's own world's size, with green and blue cloud bands that a bigger tube brings out. Six constellations are charted \u2014 the Pot, the Marrow, the Fence, the Gull, the Kettle and the Causeway \u2014 and a plate is worth something only if it holds a moon or a whole constellation; blank sky sells for nothing \u2014 and the 20mm aperture resolves 5.8 arcseconds, so every image is a little soft. Overcast is worth nothing \u2014 a plate taken at cloud five is a photograph of cloud. Aperture is more than half of what the rest is worth: the weather and the magnification help, but a bigger tube is the only way to reach the top of the price bands. All three are visible through the eyepiece. A car will kill him — they do not brake for plants. Every world starts at seven in the morning. Doba must sleep every night: if he is still awake at two in the morning he does not survive it, wherever he happens to be. Bedtime matters: turn in at seven in the evening and Doba moves at 150% the next day, eight at 130%, nine at 100%, ten at 85%, eleven at 70%, midnight at 60%, one at 50%. Two in the morning and he does not wake up. Indoors he walks on the keys or to wherever you click on the floor, the doorway included. Click something and he walks over to it before he touches it — nothing works from across the room. T looks at the phone on foot. On foot, Q and E turn the camera and R points it north again; at the wheel, R and F turn it, Q and E change gear, and Z brings up the phone. The desk and television aren't wired up yet; the bed sleeps him through to seven and restores his breath and running. The world is cut into twenty-four timezones, each keeping the time of its own meridian, so the hour jumps by one as Doba crosses a boundary and the clock flashes. The label counts the hours between where he is and home. A full day passes in ten minutes. Night sweeps across the world from west to east, so half of it is dark at any moment and anywhere you stand gets five minutes of night in every ten. Street lamps, lit windows and doorway lights come on as it passes. Where a grid cell falls off the edge of town it is too small to carry a full block, so it becomes a suburb plot instead: a handful of pots like Doba's in other shades of terracotta, turned so their doors face the street. Two islands carry towns, far apart, joined by a four-lane motorway, two lanes each way either side of a central reservation, with traffic on it at forty miles an hour; it bridges the sea where it must and shows up on the map; only the one nearest Doba has a shop. Each is about a hundred and fifty metres across, its houses the same size as Doba's pot: a grid of blocks turned to a random angle, with the streets left as the gaps between them. Buildings are solid, so you walk the roads. Every junction has signals on a fifteen-second cycle with zebra crossings on each approach. Cars keep to the centre lines, stop on red, take turnings at junctions, leave the lane to Doba's house alone, watch the road ahead of them whichever street it belongs to, queue behind one another and other snappers use the pavements. A lane runs out of town along one of the street axes, and Doba's pot sits beside it with the door onto the road, and any block that would have landed in his garden is cleared away. A food shop stands in town, a quarter-turn round from the lane home and half as far out. Away from the towns, half a dozen snapper villages sit alone \u2014 built the same way as the ring of suburb plots round a town, just a small cross of streets about a sixth the size, all pot houses and no shops or blocks \u2014 each reached by a country road that winds out from the nearest street or the motorway. Off any proper road the car is limited to half speed; on a country road it can do three quarters. Wild regions are scattered over the rest of the land. Thickets are packed with bushes sharing one colour. Clearings have no bushes, a lighter ground, and clumps of flowers. Rocky ground gets a dull patch and a scatter of stones. Scrub is mostly grass.

When the house is off screen, a marker pins itself to the edge pointing the way, with the distance in metres — fifty units to the metre. Every map picks a prevailing wind and the grass leans with it. Doba walks at eight miles an hour and runs at fifteen — hold shift or press Run. He has twenty-five seconds of running in him and needs thirty-five to get it back. He swims at half whichever pace he is on and only for twenty seconds before he has to wash back ashore. The edges of the world join up — swim off one side and you arrive on the other.
